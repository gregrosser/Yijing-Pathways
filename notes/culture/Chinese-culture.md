# Chinese Culture

An attempt to grasp the most important streams shaping Chinese culture,
from the earliest archaeological evidence to the present. Compiled from
sourced research (session of 2026-08-09); claims below have been checked
against reference sources rather than asserted from memory, and marked
where a common claim turned out to be an oversimplification.

## Timeline overview

| Period | Dates | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Yangshao culture** | c. 5000 – 3000 BCE | Painted pottery, middle Yellow River |
| **Hongshan culture** | c. 4700 – 2900 BCE | Jade-working, ritual platforms; overlaps Yangshao geographically to the northeast |
| **Longshan culture** | c. 3000 – 1900 BCE | Black pottery; direct successor to Yangshao, immediately precedes Xia |
| **Xia dynasty** | c. 2070 – 1600 BCE | Traditional first dynasty; semi-legendary, no confirmed writing found yet |
| **Shang dynasty** | c. 1600 – 1045 BCE | Earliest dynasty with solid archaeological + written confirmation (oracle bones); ancestor worship, early divination |
| **Western Zhou** | 1045 – 771 BCE | The ritual/political order Confucius would later idealize; Duke of Zhou (11th c. BCE) is his explicit model |
| — Spring and Autumn period | 770 – 476 BCE | **Confucius** (c. 551–479 BCE); also **Laozi** (traditionally 6th c. BCE, historicity debated) |
| — Warring States period | 475 – 221 BCE | **Mencius** (c. 371–289 BCE), **Zhuangzi** (c. 369–286 BCE), **Xunzi** (c. 310–after 238 BCE) — the "hundred schools" era, the most concentrated burst of philosophical activity in Chinese history |
| **Qin dynasty** | 221 – 206 BCE | First unified empire; Legalist state philosophy, burned rival texts including Confucian ones |
| **Han dynasty** | 206 BCE – 220 CE | **Dong Zhongshu** (c. 179–104 BCE) installs Confucianism as state orthodoxy, 136 BCE — holds for ~2,000 years; Buddhism enters from India, roughly 1st century CE |
| **Three Kingdoms** | 220 – 280 CE | Fragmentation after Han collapse |
| **Jin dynasty** | 265 – 420 CE | Buddhism spreading and taking root |
| **Northern and Southern dynasties** | 420 – 589 CE | Buddhism's major growth period; north/south political split |
| **Sui dynasty** | 581 – 618 CE | Brief reunification |
| **Tang dynasty** | 618 – 907 CE | Buddhism's cultural high point; term *Sanjiao* ("Three Teachings") crystallizes — Confucianism, Daoism, Buddhism formally named as a triad |
| **Song dynasty** | 960 – 1279 CE | **Zhu Xi** (1130–1200 CE) reformulates Confucianism as Neo-Confucianism, partly in response to Buddhist metaphysics; *Sanjiao* actively promoted as harmonious synthesis |
| **Yuan dynasty** | 1271 – 1368 CE | Mongol rule |
| **Ming dynasty** | 1368 – 1644 CE | Neo-Confucian orthodoxy consolidated |
| **Qing dynasty** | 1644 – 1912 CE | Last imperial dynasty, Manchu rule |
| **Republic of China** | 1912 – 1949 | End of the imperial system |
| **People's Republic of China** | 1949 – present | Includes the 1950s ethnographic surveys that produced the current 56-ethnic-group classification |

Shape worth noting: the "hundred schools" cluster (Confucius through Xunzi,
plus Laozi/Zhuangzi) spans only a few centuries. Everything after is far
more spread out — roughly 300 years to Dong Zhongshu, then a 1,200-year gap
to Zhu Xi, whose Neo-Confucianism is chronologically closer to the present
than to Confucius himself.

## The "Three Teachings" — a working framework, with real caveats

Chinese culture is very often summarized via three streams — Confucianism,
Daoism, Buddhism — and this is a genuine native framework, not just an
outsider's simplification. The term *Sanjiao* (三教, "Three Teachings")
crystallized in the Tang dynasty and was actively promoted as *Sanjiao
Heyi* ("Three Teachings Harmonious as One") in the Song. But it has two
real gaps:

- **Buddhism is not indigenous.** It arrived from India around the 1st
  century CE — Confucianism and Daoism are the two genuinely indigenous
  streams, described in reference sources as "the two great indigenous
  philosophical traditions of China," originating around the same time
  (6th–5th century BCE) in neighboring regions.
- **Folk religion sits underneath/around all three**, not fully captured
  by any of them — ancestor worship, local deity cults, and shamanic
  practice going back to Neolithic cultures such as Hongshan. Each of the
  three teachings absorbed pieces of this substrate rather than replacing
  it, and it remains arguably as influential in everyday practice as any
  named school.

### Confucianism (Ru, 儒)

**Did not originate with Confucius.** The term *Ru* predates him —
originally ritual/music specialists, possibly disinherited former Shang or
Zhou aristocrats, traceable to the Shang dynasty. Confucius said of
himself: "I transmit and do not author" (述而不作). His explicit model was
the Duke of Zhou, roughly 500 years before his own time. His real
innovation, per the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, was theoretical
rather than institutional — relocating the *rationale* for existing rites
and music toward interior ethical states rather than external duty.

The tradition kept developing for over two millennia after him: **Mencius**
(innate human goodness) and **Xunzi** (goodness must be trained in) argued
opposite readings of Confucius within a century of each other; **Dong
Zhongshu** got Confucianism installed as Han state orthodoxy (136 BCE), a
political rather than philosophical turning point; **Zhu Xi**, 1,200+ years
later, reformulated it as Neo-Confucianism, adding metaphysics partly in
response to Buddhism. "Ruism is not the thought of a single person but a
living scholarly community spanning 2,500 years."

### Daoism

Traditionally traced to **Laozi** (6th century BCE, historicity debated)
and **Zhuangzi** (c. 369–286 BCE). Equally indigenous to Confucianism, not
more so — the claim that "Daoist" can stand broadly for "original Chinese
culture" doesn't hold up as historical equivalence, though it has a loose
basis: Daoism absorbed and systematized much of the older yin-yang/folk
cosmological substrate more thoroughly than Confucianism did, which is
probably why the shorthand persists colloquially.

**Trigram gender in the Bagua** (relevant to the Yijing work this document
sits alongside): the traditional yang/yin classification of the eight
trigrams (Qian/Zhen/Kan/Gen = yang "sons"; Kun/Xun/Li/Dui = yin
"daughters") is determined by the *minority-line count* (a single yang
line, or all three, = yang; a single yin line, or none, = yin) — not by
the value of the first/bottom line specifically. Line *position*
(bottom/middle/top) determines only birth order within a gender, not
gender itself. Verified by direct computation against all eight trigrams:
the minority-line rule matches the traditional classification 8/8; a
bottom-line rule would only match 4/8.

**Daoist numerology**: confirmed — odd numbers (1,3,5,7,9) are yang, even
numbers (2,4,6,8) are yin. Double-odd calendar dates (5/5, 7/7, etc.) were
considered *too* yang and thus inauspicious, which is why counterbalancing
festivals were placed on them.

### Buddhism

Arrived from India c. 1st century CE (Han dynasty), spread through the
Three Kingdoms/Jin/Northern-Southern-dynasties period, reached its
Chinese cultural peak in the Tang. Its rise is what precipitated both the
*Sanjiao* framework (Tang) and Zhu Xi's metaphysical reformulation of
Confucianism (Song) — i.e. two of the other streams' most significant
later developments were reactions to Buddhism's presence, not independent
of it.

## Beyond the three teachings: ethnic and cultural diversity

China today recognizes 56 ethnic groups — Han (~91.6% of the population)
plus 55 minority groups — spread mainly across the west, southwest, and
northwest (Yunnan, Guizhou, Guangxi, Tibet, Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia,
and elsewhere). The Han majority itself is not culturally uniform, spanning
many regional cultures, dialects, and traditions.

Worth flagging: the specific *56-group* classification is a mid-20th-century
administrative project — 1950s ethnographic surveys, language mapping, and
field research, influenced by Soviet-style classification systems — not
itself ancient. The underlying fact of deep, long-standing ethnic and
cultural diversity (Tibetan, Uyghur, Mongol, Manchu, Zhuang, Miao, and many
more, each historically distinct) long predates that classification effort
and long predates the present political boundaries.

## Open threads

- Legalism (the Qin state philosophy) and Mohism are absent from the
  "three teachings" framing entirely, despite having shaped imperial
  governance and early philosophical debate significantly — not yet
  researched in depth here.
- Regional/local folk religion is gestured at above but not explored on
  its own terms — it deserves treatment as a fourth stream in its own
  right rather than as a residual category.
- The relationship between Daoist philosophy (Laozi/Zhuangzi,
  *daojia* 道家) and religious/institutional Daoism (*daojiao* 道教,
  organized religion with priesthoods, pantheons, alchemy) is real and
  significant but not yet distinguished in this document — currently
  "Daoism" above conflates the two.
