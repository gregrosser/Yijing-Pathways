# Fuxi Trigram/Hexagram Sequence — Disputed Origins

Research note (2026-08-09) on the dating and origins of the Fuxi trigram
and hexagram material, covering traditional/legendary claims, archaeological
evidence, textual scholarship, and — most relevant to this project — the
actual origin of the Xiantian ("Earlier Heaven") circular arrangement this
project's Fu Xi circle work is built on.

## Key finding for this project (revised 2026-08-09)

**Shao Yong did not invent the Xiantian diagram whole-cloth in the 11th
century — he was the point at which a diagram already circulating in a
restricted Daoist lineage for at least a century became public.** The
*History of Song* records a named four-generation transmission chain:
Chen Tuan → Chong Fang → Mu Xiu → Li Zhicai → Shao Yong. Chen Tuan
(c. 906–989 CE) was a Daoist recluse on Mount Hua associated with Internal
Alchemy (Neidan) — a school built on exactly the kind of closed,
master-to-initiate transmission this note's "elite secrecy" discussion
below addresses directly. Some scholarship goes further still, suggesting
Chen Tuan was not the originator either, and that Han-dynasty image-number
scholar Jing Fang (77–37 BCE) had already worked with related "innate"
eight-trigram material — a live, if less certain, scholarly thread that
would push conceptual roots back roughly a millennium before Shao Yong.
Shao Yong's own contribution was framed, and should be read, as
*systematizing and publishing* an existing restricted-lineage diagram,
not authoring one from nothing. This is a materially different claim from
this note's original framing (below), which treated Shao Yong's
11th-century activity as the diagram's actual origin point.

None of this reaches all the way back to the legendary Fuxi date of 2852
BCE — that gap is real and unclosed. See "Elite secrecy and restricted
transmission" below for the fuller argument and evidence.

So — with that revision in mind — four objects, of very different and
still-debated ages, currently travel under the single name "Fuxi":

| Object | Traditional claim | Actual evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Numerical divination practice | — | Archaeologically attested, oracle bones/bronzes, c. 1050 BCE |
| The Zhouyi text itself | King Wen / Duke of Zhou, 11th c. BCE | Linguistic dating: c. 820s BCE (Shaughnessy) |
| 64-hexagram *ordering* | King Wen sequence, same era | Unstable for centuries — Mawangdui (168 BCE) and Shanghai Museum (Warring States) manuscripts each show different orders; "the reasoning, if any, that informs [the King Wen] sequence is unknown" |
| Xiantian/Fuxi *circular* arrangement | Fuxi, c. 2852 BCE | Documented restricted transmission from Chen Tuan (d. 989 CE) onward; published by Shao Yong, 11th century CE; possible (less certain) Han-dynasty roots via Jing Fang, 1st c. BCE |

## Traditional / legendary claims

**Fuxi and the trigrams.** The origin story comes from the *Xici* ("Great
Commentary," one of the Ten Wings appended to the Yijing): *"In ancient
times, when Bao Xi [Fuxi] ruled the world, he looked up and observed the
images in the heavens, looked down and observed the patterns on earth. He
observed the markings of birds and beasts and the suitability of the
land"* — from which he deduced the trigrams, aided by the legendary
"Dragon Horse" River Diagram (He Tu). Traditional chronology places this
around **2852 BCE**, with Fuxi as the first of the mythical "Three
Sovereigns." Same mythological cycle credits him with fishing nets,
cooking, domestication of animals, music, and writing — a general
"culture hero" figure, not a narrowly specific claim about trigrams alone.

**King Wen and the hexagrams.** Tradition holds King Wen (11th century
BCE, while imprisoned by the last Shang king) arranged the 64 hexagrams
and wrote the hexagram judgments, with his son the Duke of Zhou adding the
line texts, and Confucius's school later adding the Ten Wings
commentaries.

**Modern scholarly view of Fuxi himself.** Treated as a legendary
chieftain / cultural symbol, not a historical individual — *"Archaeological
evidence for Fuxi remains elusive, with no direct artifacts attributable
to his era."* The Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors cycle generally is
understood as myth encoding the *idea* of civilization's origins, not
literal history.

## Archaeological evidence: numerical trigrams/hexagrams

Sets of numerals (digits like 1, 5, 6, 7, 8 — not yet the abstract
broken/unbroken *line* notation familiar today) appear on **late Shang and
early Western Zhou oracle bones and bronzes**, dating to **roughly 1050
BCE** onward — right at the Shang–Zhou transition. Discovery history is
well documented: Li Xueqin first guessed a connection to the Book of
Changes' number system in 1956, Tang Lan identified most of the numerals
in 1957, and Zhang Zhenglang's 1980 paper formally established the
"numerical hexagram" interpretation (first presented at a 1978 Changchun
paleography conference). This suggests the yin/yang *line* symbolism most
people picture may be a later abstraction of an originally purely
numerical divination practice.

## Textual dating of the Zhouyi itself

This is where traditional attribution and modern scholarship diverge most
sharply. Edward Shaughnessy's linguistic and literary analysis places the
Zhouyi's actual composition **in the last two decades of the 9th century
BCE, during King Xuan's reign** — 150–200 years *later* than the
traditional King Wen/Duke of Zhou attribution. Even the King Wen
*sequence* specifically (the ordering of the 64 hexagrams) has no
established rationale — one specialist source states plainly, *"the
reasoning, if any, that informs this sequence is unknown."*

Manuscript evidence complicates the picture further: the **Mawangdui silk
manuscript** (buried 168 BCE, likely copied in the 170s BCE) preserves a
*different* hexagram order than the received King Wen sequence — proof the
ordering wasn't fixed or universally agreed on even in the early Han. The
**Shanghai Museum Chu bamboo slips** (Warring States era, ~4th–3rd century
BCE) preserve yet another textual witness, still under active philological
study for variants.

## Elite secrecy and restricted transmission

This section responds directly to a specific line of argument raised in
discussion (2026-08-09): that all cultures are stratified into elite,
middle, and deprived strata; that elite sub-cultures characteristically
protect their "ways" through secrecy and obfuscation, refusing entry to
outsiders while permitting public display of high-culture artifacts that
cannot themselves serve as entry tickets to the elite (e.g. royal
ceremonies — public spectacle imbued with restricted values); and that
given 5,000 years of Chinese cultural development, it would be surprising
if *no* such elite secrecy applied to trigram/hexagram material. The
question posed was: can known archaeology and research tell us anything
concrete about what such secrets were?

The general framework has real scholarly grounding — the
exoteric/esoteric distinction across traditions (Greek mystery cults,
Kabbalah, Sufi orders, craft guilds), James C. Scott's "public transcript
vs. hidden transcript," Bourdieu's account of cultural capital as
embodied and non-transferable. Applied to this specific material, three
concrete findings support a moderated version of the argument:

**1. The Chen Tuan lineage is real, named, and documented in official
history**, not inferred. Chen Tuan (c. 906–989 CE), a Daoist recluse on
Mount Hua who repeatedly refused imperial appointment, is recorded as the
source of a four-generation closed transmission of the Xiantian diagram,
reaching Shao Yong only at the final link. Chen Tuan's own association
with Neidan (Internal Alchemy) places this squarely inside a tradition
built on restricted, coded, initiate-only transmission by design — this
is not a retrofitted interpretation, it is what Neidan lineages
explicitly do. Some scholarship holds Chen Tuan was himself a transmitter
rather than originator, pointing to Han-dynasty scholar Jing Fang
(77–37 BCE) as having worked with related material — unconfirmed by a
firm primary source so far, but a serious, live scholarly position rather
than fringe speculation.

**2. Archaeology has already, concretely, vindicated one "lost" text of
exactly this kind.** The *Guicang* (歸藏), one of the "Three Changes"
(三易) divination systems alongside the *Lianshan* and the *Zhouyi* —
traditionally assigned to the Shang dynasty, known for most of history
only through scattered quotation, and **"long despised as pure
invention"** by mainstream scholarship — was directly confirmed by the
1993 excavation of Qin-dynasty bamboo slips at Wangjiatai (Tomb 15): over
4,000 characters, roughly 54 distinct hexagram figures. A text dismissed
as fabricated was proven genuine by physical excavation. This is a direct
existence proof, not a hypothetical: material presumed spurious or
irretrievably lost *has* been recovered and validated before, for
precisely this category of object.

**3. Divinatory knowledge was institutionally restricted from very
early on.** The Zhou court's Offices of Spring housed a Grand Diviner
(太卜, Taibu), formally responsible for all three of the Three Changes
systems, working alongside a Director of Incantation and a Director of
Astronomy — a professionalized, state-controlled office, not open
practice. This gives the elite-secrecy hypothesis structural plausibility
independent of the Chen Tuan case specifically.

**Honest limits.** The Chen Tuan lineage reaches back with real names to
roughly 900 CE; the more speculative Jing Fang thread would reach to the
1st century BCE if confirmed. Neither closes the gap to the legendary
Fuxi date of 2852 BCE — that remains a roughly 2,800-year (Jing Fang
thread) to 3,800-year (Chen Tuan only) unexplained span. What has changed
is not that the gap is closed, but that "Shao Yong invented this in the
11th century" is no longer the accurate summary — the accurate summary is
that a documented restricted lineage existed before him, the Guicang case
proves such recovery is achievable in principle for this category of
material, and the door is open rather than closed on how much further
back the Xiantian material's real roots go.

## Implications for this project

- The Fu Xi trigram circle and 64-hexagram circle already derived and
  verified in this project (see `whats-next.md`, session of 2026-08-06)
  are a faithful reconstruction of the diagram as **published by Shao
  Yong**, which the evidence above indicates he received via a documented
  Daoist restricted lineage (Chen Tuan onward, c. 900s CE) rather than
  invented himself — and which may have roots extending further back
  still (Jing Fang thread, unconfirmed). Worth being precise about this
  in any public-facing framing (the app itself, documentation, etc.):
  "Fu Xi sequence" is the *traditional name* for a diagram whose earliest
  solid documentation is Song-dynasty publication of a pre-existing
  restricted transmission — not literally Fuxi's own work, but also not
  simply an 11th-century invention.
- The odd/even (yang/yin) numbering convention this project adopted for
  the 64-hexagram circle (left half odd 1–63, right half even 2–64) sits
  comfortably downstream of this — it's the project's own numbering
  choice layered onto the published diagram, not something independently
  attested in the historical record.
- The Guicang precedent (§ Elite secrecy, above) is worth keeping in mind
  as a live possibility rather than closing the question: material
  currently dismissed as spurious or lost has, at least once, been
  concretely recovered by excavation for exactly this category of
  Yijing-adjacent divinatory object.
- If the project ever wants to represent "how old is this really,"
  the honest layered answer is: real numerical divination practice
  (archaeological, ~1050 BCE) → the Zhouyi text (~820s BCE, per
  Shaughnessy) → hexagram ordering unstable for centuries after that →
  the Xiantian diagram in documented restricted transmission from at
  least c. 900s CE (Chen Tuan) → published by Shao Yong, 1011–1077 CE.

## Position

The following is Greg's own considered view, stated in his words rather
than derived from the research above — distinct from, though informed by,
the findings in this note.

- The consensus opinion on the Xiantian trigram and hexagram cultural
  artifacts is understood. Historians must be able to defend their
  positions on the ground of extant texts — that is a real and reasonable
  constraint on the discipline, not a failure of it.
- It is at least possible that the Xiantian cultural artifacts predate the
  extant documents, in order to protect elites and their secrets.
